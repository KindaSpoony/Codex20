AGENTS.md

Introduction: In the intelligence community, analysis is often a team effort with individuals specializing in different aspects of the intelligence cycle. The OSINT Playbook reflects this by assigning notional roles or agent archetypes to cover the end-to-end workflow of open-source investigations. Defining roles ensures that all critical responsibilities – from information gathering to quality control – are addressed, even if in practice one person or an automated system wears multiple hats. This section describes each role’s responsibilities and how they work together, drawing on traditional intelligence analyst archetypes (the kinds of roles you’d find in agencies) adapted for OSINT.

Roles and Responsibilities:
	•	OSINT Collection Officer (Collector): This role is responsible for gathering information from all relevant open sources. Modeled after the collection managers and field intelligence officers in classic intel units, the Collector ensures the investigation has enough raw data to analyze. Key duties include:
	•	Scoping and requirements definition: working with the analyst(s) to understand what information is needed (e.g. if the mission is Identity Verification, the Collector determines which databases, social platforms, or records to check).
	•	Using a wide array of OSINT tools and sources to retrieve data: web search engines, social media, public records, news archives, GIS tools for geospatial data, etc. The Collector leverages advanced search strategies (such as specialized queries, site-specific searches, API endpoints) to find hard-to-get information.
	•	Ethical compliance and logging: abiding by legal and ethical guidelines during collection (no hacking or accessing non-public info, in line with the playbook’s directives ) and recording source details for everything gathered. This logging enables verification and citation later, and is crucial for transparency.
	•	First-pass triage: filtering out obviously irrelevant or low-quality sources early (for example, discarding blatant disinformation or duplicates), while making sure not to inadvertently toss useful intelligence. However, final judgments on relevance are left to the analysis phase; the Collector’s focus is breadth and completeness.
	•	In an automated context, the Collector agent might be a crawler or aggregator module that fetches data under defined parameters, respecting rate limits and using APIs. In a human team, the Collector might be an investigator skilled in online research.
	•	OSINT Analyst (All-Source Analyst): The Analyst is the central sense-maker who turns collected data into meaningful insights. In intelligence agencies, an all-source analyst fuses information from various collection disciplines; here, our OSINT Analyst focuses on correlating open sources (which may include many types of media). Responsibilities include:
	•	Information synthesis and analysis: reviewing the raw data collected and identifying key facts, patterns, and anomalies. The Analyst uses critical thinking and techniques like link analysis, timeline building, and comparative analysis to piece together a coherent narrative or assessment of the situation.
	•	Hypothesis generation and testing: forming hypotheses about what might be going on (for example, “Are these accounts part of a coordinated influence campaign?” or “Which of these news reports is accurate regarding event timing?”) and then looking for evidence in the data to support or refute these hypotheses. They might employ structured analytic techniques such as Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH) or scenario analysis to remain objective (see Heuristics.md for details on these techniques).
	•	Threat analysis and contextualization: if the mission involves threat detection or understanding implications, the Analyst evaluates indicators of malicious activity within the data . They put findings in context with geopolitical or social knowledge (e.g. understanding that a piece of propaganda aligns with known tactics of a certain group).
	•	Iterative querying: The Analyst often goes back to the Collector with refined requirements as analysis reveals gaps. For instance, if during analysis it becomes clear that a certain detail is missing or a conflicting account needs resolution, the Analyst will task the Collector (or an automated collection function) to fetch more data or look into specific sources.
	•	Maintaining objectivity: A crucial part of the Analyst’s role is to stay unbiased. They must be wary of jumping to conclusions and instead let evidence lead. By documenting analytic reasoning and considering alternatives, the Analyst upholds tradecraft standards of impartiality and rigor.
	•	Validation & Verification Specialist: This role serves as the quality control and fact-checker of the team. In the IC, this function might not always be a separate person (analysts themselves do evaluation), but our playbook highlights it as a distinct role to ensure rigorous source vetting. The Verification Specialist’s tasks include:
	•	Source credibility assessment: reviewing each source collected and rating its reliability according to the Tier criteria defined in the playbook (Tier 1/2/3) . They double-check the provenance of information (e.g., is that “news site” actually a known biased propaganda outlet? Is that social media post coming from a bot or an authentic profile?).
	•	Cross-verification of facts: for every key fact or piece of evidence that the Analyst wants to use, the Verification Specialist checks if it appears in independent sources. They make sure the “two-source rule” is satisfied for critical facts . If something only appears once, they flag it as needing corroboration or at least clearly marking it as unverified in the report.
	•	Bias and error checking: analyzing whether a source might have particular biases or if there are translation errors/misquotes in the information. They document any such caveats (e.g. “Source X is a political blog with a known lean; use with caution”) so the Analyst and final report can account for those .
	•	Maintain the audit trail: the Verification Specialist ensures that for every claim in the analysis, there is a traceable reference in the collected material. They might maintain a matrix or table of facts vs. sources (similar to how intelligence analysts keep track of evidence supporting each hypothesis).
	•	In coordinated operations, this role acts as a second pair of eyes – a bit like a peer reviewer – who can independently validate findings. In an automated setup, this could be an algorithmic process that flags low-confidence info, but human oversight at this stage is extremely valuable due to the nuance in assessing credibility.
	•	Intelligence Reports Officer (Reporting Lead): This role is tasked with producing the final intelligence product and ensuring it meets the end-user’s needs (be it a policy maker, security team, or the public). In traditional settings, a reports officer or team lead compiles analysts’ work into polished reports. Here, the Reporting Lead:
	•	Structures the report: They decide the best way to present findings logically – e.g., chronology of events, by sub-topic, or by hypothesis – so that a reader can easily follow the conclusions. They use the playbook’s guidance to include all necessary components: key findings, source citations, confidence levels, and notes on any unresolved questions .
	•	Edits for clarity and consistency: The Reporting Lead ensures the language is clear, jargon-free (or explained), and that the tone remains objective and professional. Emotional or sensational language from sources is filtered out; the report focuses on verified facts and their implications .
	•	Cites sources and evidence: Every significant piece of information in the report should trace back to the collected data. The Reporting role double-checks that citations are present and formatted correctly, and that any direct quotes are accurate. This builds trust with the reader that the claims are backed by evidence.
	•	Integrates confidence and caveats: The Reporting Lead prominently notes the confidence level of each conclusion (High/Med/Low) as determined in the analysis stage . They also include caveats where appropriate, such as: “Two sources conflict on this detail; we have marked it as unresolved” or “This assessment relies on a social media post of unconfirmed origin (Tier 3 source), so confidence is low.” By doing so, the final product is transparent about its strengths and limitations, which is a hallmark of good tradecraft.
	•	Final quality assurance: Before dissemination, the Reporting Lead (potentially with the Team Lead) reviews the entire investigation for any gaps. Is every requirement from the mission addressed? Have all high-threat findings been handled according to escalation rules? Is the overall analytic line sound and supported by the evidence? This is the last check to ensure the intelligence is actionable and meets integrity standards.
	•	Team Lead / Coordinator: In larger or more sensitive operations, a Team Lead oversees the process and ensures coordination among all the above roles. This archetype is akin to an intelligence supervisor or project manager. Key contributions of the Team Lead:
	•	Mission planning: at the outset, the Lead helps translate the intelligence requirement (the question to be answered) into a concrete plan. They assign tasks to the Collector, Analyst, etc., or configure the autonomous agent roles if using an automated system.
	•	Monitoring progress and compliance: the Lead tracks the investigation’s progress through the stages. They ensure that the playbook’s guidelines are followed at each step (for example, making sure the Verification Specialist has checked source tiers before the Analyst moves on to writing conclusions).
	•	Decision on escalations: if the Collector or Analyst flags something as high risk (e.g. a credible threat or a major discovery that might need immediate action), the Team Lead decides how to handle it. Following the escalation rules, they might pull in additional human experts or notify authorities, and they document these actions .
	•	Inter-role coordination: the Lead facilitates communication. For instance, if the Analyst needs more data, the Lead authorizes the Collector to dedicate more time or resources there. If the Verification Specialist finds a major issue with a source, the Lead may refocus the Analyst accordingly. In essence, this role keeps the parts moving in harmony and resolves any conflicts or bottlenecks.
	•	Mentorship and tradecraft enforcement: the Team Lead, being experienced, also mentors junior analysts or fine-tunes the automated system’s parameters to uphold tradecraft. If an analyst is fixated on one hypothesis, the Lead might encourage a red team review (have someone else challenge that hypothesis) to avoid tunnel vision. If using an AI agent, the Lead ensures it’s configured to present alternative hypotheses or doubt when appropriate.

Coordination and Workflow: The above roles are distinct in responsibility but tightly interconnected in practice. A successful OSINT investigation is iterative and collaborative:
	•	The Collector and Analyst often work in tandem: as the Collector brings in data, the Analyst provides feedback on what is or isn’t helpful, refining the collection focus continuously. They might have daily (or real-time) syncs to adjust search terms or target new sources as leads emerge.
	•	The Verification Specialist operates in parallel once data starts coming in, tagging source reliability and verifying early findings. This early verification can prevent wasted effort (e.g., flagging a dubious source before the Analyst builds analysis on it). The Specialist communicates findings like “Source A and B are likely duplicates” or “Source X is not trustworthy” so the Analyst knows how much weight to give each piece of information.
	•	The Team Lead ensures that this cycle of collection-analysis-verification stays on track towards the mission goal. They might hold briefings at key milestones (after initial collection, after preliminary analysis) to have each role share updates. This is analogous to an intelligence fusion cell where different experts share insights to form a complete picture.
	•	The Reporting Officer gets involved as soon as there are substantive findings. Rather than waiting until the very end, they may start drafting sections of the report early (like background or methodology) and update it as analysis solidifies. They also communicate with the Analyst about how to frame certain findings for the intended audience (e.g., “The client is mostly interested in the credibility of that leaked document – let’s make sure we emphasize how we verified it.”).
	•	Throughout, all roles use a common log or platform (in an automated system, a shared database; in a human team, perhaps a collaborative tool or just regular meetings) so information flows seamlessly. Every piece of data, once collected and vetted, is accessible to the Analyst and Reporting Officer; every analytic note or hypothesis is visible to the Team Lead and can trigger more collection or verification as needed.

By dividing responsibilities yet fostering close coordination, the playbook ensures no aspect of the investigative process is neglected. This mirrors real-world intelligence operations where specialization increases efficiency and reliability, but teamwork brings it all together. An OSINT operation following these role guidelines will be equipped to collect widely, analyze deeply, double-check thoroughly, and report clearly – covering the full spectrum of intelligence production within a single cohesive workflow.
